# Hold the Thread

## Agency-Centered Family Care Coordination

*A minimum-viable practice for keeping an older adult's care connected without trying to become the clinician.*

Healthcare is often divided across clinics, portals, medication lists, home-health services, and family conversations. This method helps a patient and family preserve continuity, ask better questions, and make responsibility visible. The goal is not perfect documentation. It is to keep the person's preferences and lived experience at the center while making the next safe step clear.

## Six habits

### 1. Begin with the person, not the record

Ask: What matters to the patient now? What are they experiencing? What do they want help deciding? Support their voice and decision-making as fully as possible. A caregiver adds continuity; they do not speak over the person receiving care.

### 2. Keep one living summary

Use one page, notebook, or simple digital file containing:

- current medications and how they are actually taken;
- important symptoms or changes in daily function;
- upcoming appointments;
- two or three unanswered questions; and
- current next steps and who owns them.

Start small. Do not try to rebuild the entire medical record.

### 3. Label the source

Distinguish patient report, caregiver observation, clinician instruction, electronic record, home device or measurement, and AI-generated synthesis. Conflicting sources should remain visible rather than quietly becoming fact.

### 4. Compare the record with real life

Check whether medication lists, verbal instructions, home measurements, and actual practice agree. Turn discrepancies into questions: “Dad is taking 10 mg, but the portal lists 20 mg. Which dose should he take, and who will correct the record?” Do not independently resolve consequential clinical discrepancies.

### 5. Turn uncertainty into ownership

For every unresolved issue, record the question, why it matters, the responsible person, the next action, and the expected follow-up. Ask: **Who owns the next step?**

### 6. Close the loop without carrying every loop

Follow up on safety-critical items. Let lower-priority questions wait. Return responsibilities to clinics, agencies, and vendors. Family advocacy should not require heroic unpaid labor.

## A simple visit practice

**Before:** Choose two or three decisions or questions. Bring a short summary; keep supporting detail available only if needed.

**During:** Lead with a shared goal: “We want a safe, workable plan that reflects what matters to Dad.” Describe your understanding, invite correction, and distinguish observation from conclusion.

**After:** Record decisions, remaining uncertainty, owners, and timing. Confirm that important changes reached the official record.

## AI and Critical AI Health Literacy

AI can organize notes, compare records, identify possible contradictions, and prepare questions. Treat its output as synthesis to verify, not medical fact or instruction.

Ask:

- Who does this tool serve?
- Does it expand understanding and choices, or narrow them?
- What might it omit or invent?
- What sensitive information am I sharing?
- Could its framing overshadow the patient's own words?

Bring AI material into care as questions: **“This is our current understanding. What are we missing?”**

## Limits

This practice cannot repair every fragmented system. Risks include caregiver burden, privacy exposure, bias, unequal access, and shifting system failures onto one family member. Urgent safety concerns require appropriate clinical or emergency help rather than reliance on a record or AI.

> You are not training to become a medical expert. You are learning to hold the thread: preserve the patient's voice, keep sources clear, bring the right questions forward, and help responsible people close the next loop.
