# Hold the Thread

**Framework:** Agency-Centered Family Care Coordination  
**Subtitle:** A minimum-viable practice for keeping an older adult's care connected without trying to become the clinician.

## Purpose

Care is often divided across clinics, portals, medication lists, home-health services, and family conversations. Hold the Thread helps a patient and family preserve continuity, ask better questions, and make responsibility visible. The aim is not perfect documentation. It is to keep the person's preferences and lived experience at the center while making the next safe step clear.

## Six habits

1. **Begin with the person, not the record.** Ask what matters now, what the patient is experiencing, and what they want help deciding. Support their voice and decision-making as fully as possible. A caregiver adds continuity; they do not speak over the person receiving care.

2. **Keep one living summary.** Use one page, notebook, or simple digital file for medications and actual use, important symptoms or changes in daily function, upcoming appointments, two or three open questions, and current next steps with owners. Start small; do not rebuild the entire medical record.

3. **Label the source.** Distinguish patient report, caregiver observation, clinician instruction, electronic record, home device or measurement, and AI-generated synthesis. Keep conflicts visible rather than quietly turning them into fact.

4. **Compare the record with real life.** Check whether lists, verbal instructions, home measurements, and actual practice agree. Turn discrepancies into questions: "Dad takes 10 mg, but the portal lists 20 mg. Which dose should he take, and who will correct the record?" Do not independently resolve consequential clinical discrepancies.

5. **Turn uncertainty into ownership.** For each unresolved issue, note the question, why it matters, who is responsible, the next action, and expected follow-up. Close with: "Who owns the next step?"

6. **Close the loop without carrying every loop.** Follow up on safety-critical items, let lower-priority questions wait, and return responsibilities to clinics, agencies, and vendors. Family advocacy should not require heroic unpaid labor.

## A simple visit practice

- **Before:** Choose two or three decisions or questions. Bring the short summary; add supporting detail only if needed.
- **During:** Lead with a shared goal: "We want a safe, workable plan that reflects what matters to Dad." Describe your understanding, invite correction, and separate observation from conclusion.
- **After:** Record decisions, remaining uncertainty, owners, and timing. Confirm that important changes reached the official record.

## AI and critical AI health literacy

AI can organize notes, compare records, flag possible contradictions, and prepare questions. Treat its output as synthesis to verify, not as medical fact or instruction.

Ask: Who does this tool serve? Does it expand understanding and choices or narrow them? What might it omit or invent? What sensitive information am I sharing? Could its framing overshadow the patient's own words? Present AI material as questions: "This is our current understanding. What are we missing?"

## Limits and safety

This practice cannot repair every fragmented system. Risks include caregiver burden, privacy exposure, bias, unequal access, and shifting system failures onto one family member. Urgent safety concerns require appropriate clinical or emergency help, not reliance on a record or AI.

## Closing

You are not training to become a medical expert. You are learning to hold the thread: preserve the patient's voice, keep sources clear, bring the right questions forward, and help responsible people close the next loop.
